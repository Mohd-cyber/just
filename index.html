🏹Humans as Hunter-Gatherers

▶
Definition
Hunter-gatherers are societies that subsisted entirely by foraging wild plants and hunting animals, with no cultivation or animal domestication.

Key Characteristics

  • Nomadic lifestyle — followed seasonal food availability; no permanent settlements.
  • Low ecological impact — small population sizes; resource use matched regeneration rates.
  • Tool use — stone tools (Palaeolithic era), later bone and wood tools.
  • Social structure — egalitarian bands of 25–50 people; no social hierarchy.
  • Duration — approximately 95% of human evolutionary history (~2 million to ~10,000 years BCE).
Exam Point
Hunter-gatherers had a mutualistic relationship with nature — they depended on it without fundamentally altering it. This contrasts sharply with industrial society's exploitative relationship.

Mastery of Fire (~1 million years ago)

  • Cooking improved nutrition and reduced pathogens → supported larger brain development.
  • Warmth allowed colonization of colder regions.
  • Fire used for land clearing — early vegetation modification.
  • First significant human alteration of the environment.

🌾Agricultural Revolution & Ancient Civilizations

▶
Definition
The Neolithic or Agricultural Revolution (~10,000 BCE) was the transition from nomadic hunting/gathering to settled farming and pastoralism.

Environmental Impacts of Agriculture

  • Deforestation — land clearing for cultivation.
  • Soil degradation — repeated tilling, monoculture.
  • Water use — irrigation systems altered river flow.
  • Population growth — sedentary life + food surplus = population explosion.
  • Domestication — crops (wheat, rice, maize) and animals (cattle, sheep) altered ecosystems.

Environmental determinism: idea that environment shapes civilization. Environmental possibilism: humans choose how to use their environment (more accepted today).

🏰Middle Ages, Renaissance & Scientific Revolution

▶

Middle Ages (500–1400 CE)

  • Agricultural expansion — clearing of European forests (assarting).
  • Population growth → over-farming → Black Death (1347–51) — ironically, forests recovered as population collapsed.
  • Feudal systems controlled land and its use.
  • Medieval guilds regulated resource extraction.

Renaissance (14th–17th century)

  • Renewed interest in nature through art (Leonardo da Vinci's anatomical and natural studies).
  • Geographic exploration → colonialism → resource extraction from colonies.
  • Rise of anthropocentric worldview — humans as masters of nature.

Scientific Revolution (17th century)

  • Descartes, Newton — nature as a machine to be studied and controlled.
  • Francis Bacon — "knowledge is power"; advocated domination of nature.
  • Foundation laid for Industrial Revolution.

🏭Industrial Revolution & Its Environmental Impact

▶
Definition
The Industrial Revolution (c. 1760–1840, originating in Britain) marked the transition from agrarian economies to manufacturing-based economies driven by fossil fuels.

Key Environmental Impacts

  • Air pollution — coal burning → SO₂, particulates → London smog (Great Smog of 1952 killed ~12,000).
  • Water pollution — industrial effluents into rivers; Thames became heavily polluted.
  • Deforestation — timber for industry and railways.
  • Fossil fuel era — coal → oil → gas; beginning of large-scale GHG emissions.
  • Urbanization — rural→urban migration; creation of industrial cities with poor sanitation.
  • Resource extraction — mining, quarrying, large-scale agriculture.
  • Species loss — habitat destruction accelerated extinction rates.

Population Growth & Natural Resource Exploitation

  • World population: 1 billion (1804) → 2 billion (1927) → 8 billion (2022).
  • Thomas Malthus (1798) — population grows geometrically, food arithmetically → inevitable resource crisis.
  • Green Revolution (1960s) — temporarily averted Malthusian crisis but increased chemical use.
  • Overconsumption = ecological footprint exceeding Earth's carrying capacity.
Exam Fact
The Ecological Footprint measures human demand on nature. Currently, humanity uses 1.7 Earths annually — meaning we are in ecological overshoot.

🌍Global Environmental Change

▶

Major Forms of Global Environmental Change

  • Climate change — rising temperatures due to GHG emissions.
  • Ozone depletion — CFCs destroying stratospheric ozone layer.
  • Biodiversity loss — 6th mass extinction, currently ~1,000× background extinction rate.
  • Land degradation — desertification, soil erosion, salinization.
  • Freshwater depletion — groundwater over-extraction, river flow reduction.
  • Ocean acidification — CO₂ absorbed by oceans → pH decrease → coral bleaching.
  • Deforestation — ~15 billion trees cut per year; Amazon and tropical forests at risk.
Planetary Boundaries
Johan Rockström (2009) identified 9 planetary boundaries — safe operating limits for Earth systems. Climate change, biosphere integrity, and land-system change have already been transgressed.

  • Mineral wealth: India rich in coal, iron ore, mica, bauxite but mining causes significant degradation.

🦋Biodiversity — Levels, Types & India's Position

▶
Definition — CBD 1992
Biodiversity is "the variability among living organisms from all sources including terrestrial, marine, and other aquatic ecosystems and the ecological complexes of which they are part."

Levels of Biodiversity

  • Genetic diversity — variation in genes within a species (e.g., rice varieties in India — 50,000+ cultivars).
  • Species diversity — variety of species in a habitat. Measured by species richness and species evenness.
  • Ecosystem diversity — variety of habitats, biotic communities, and ecological processes (deserts, wetlands, forests).

India's Biodiversity Status

  • India is a mega-diverse country — one of 17 such countries globally.
  • ~8% of world's species in 2.4% of land area.
  • ~47,000 plant species; ~91,000 animal species recorded.
  • India is center of origin for rice, sugarcane, mango, turmeric, cotton.
  • Endemism: ~33% of India's flora is endemic.
Shannon Index & Simpson Index
Two key measures of biodiversity: Shannon-Wiener Index (H') accounts for richness + evenness; Simpson's Index (D) measures dominance.

🗺️Biodiversity Hotspots

▶
Definition — Norman Myers, 1988
A biodiversity hotspot must have ≥1,500 endemic vascular plant species AND must have lost ≥70% of its original habitat.

Global Hotspots

36 hotspots globally (as of 2016), covering only 2.5% of Earth's land area but containing >60% of the world's plant, bird, mammal, reptile, and amphibian species as endemics.

⚙️Ecosystem Services & Classification

▶
Definition — MEA 2005
Ecosystem services are the benefits people obtain from ecosystems. Classified by the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005).

Four Categories (MEA Framework)

  • Provisioning Services — food, water, timber, fiber, genetic resources, medicines.
  • Regulating Services — climate regulation, flood control, water purification, pollination, disease regulation.
  • Cultural Services — recreation, spiritual value, aesthetics, ecotourism.
  • Supporting Services — nutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production (base for all other services).
Economic Value
Robert Costanza et al. (1997) estimated global ecosystem services at $33 trillion/year (revised to $125 trillion in 2014) — far exceeding global GDP.

⚠️Threats to Biodiversity & Conservation Approaches

▶

Major Threats (HIPPO acronym)

  • Habitat destruction — land use change; most important threat globally.
  • Invasive species — e.g., Lantana camara, Water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes) in India.
  • Pollution — pesticides, heavy metals, plastic, eutrophication.
  • Population growth — overconsumption, urbanization.
  • Over-exploitation — overfishing, poaching, illegal wildlife trade.
  • Climate change — increasingly the 6th major threat.

Conservation Strategies

In-situ Conservation
Protection in the natural habitat.
  • National Parks — no human activity allowed; India has 106 National Parks.
  • Wildlife Sanctuaries — limited human activity; India has 565 sanctuaries.
  • Biosphere Reserves — UNESCO programme; India has 18 (12 in UNESCO network).
  • Tiger Reserves — Project Tiger (1973); 53 reserves currently.
  • Sacred Groves (Devaravana) — traditional community conservation.
  • Ramsar Wetlands — India has 75 Ramsar sites (highest in world as of 2022).
Ex-situ Conservation
Protection outside natural habitat.
  • Zoos — captive breeding programs; Central Zoo Authority oversees India's 150+ zoos.
  • Botanical Gardens — Howrah (oldest in Asia, 1787), Ooty, Pondicherry.
  • Seed Banks — National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources (NBPGR), Delhi; Svalbard Global Seed Vault.
  • Gene Banks, Cryopreservation — storage of genetic material at –196°C.
  • Tissue Culture — in vitro propagation of rare species.

🏗️Pollution — Basics, Production & Waste

▶
Definition
Pollution is the introduction of contaminants into the natural environment that cause adverse changes. A pollutant is a substance or energy introduced into the environment with undesired effects.

Types of Pollutants

  • Biodegradable — can be broken down by microorganisms (sewage, paper). But in excess, cause eutrophication.
  • Non-biodegradable — persist in environment (plastics, DDT, heavy metals). Undergo biomagnification.
  • Point sources — identifiable single source (factory pipe, vehicle exhaust).
  • Non-point sources — diffuse, cannot be traced to single location (agricultural runoff, urban stormwater).
Assimilative Capacity
The environment's ability to absorb and neutralize pollutants without significant harm. Pollution occurs when pollution load exceeds assimilative capacity.

💨Air Pollution

▶

Sources

  • Natural — volcanoes, dust storms, forest fires, pollen.
  • Anthropogenic — industries, vehicles, power plants, agriculture (NH₃ from fertilizers).

Primary vs. Secondary Pollutants

Primary (emitted directly)Secondary (formed in atmosphere)
SO₂, NO, CO, PM, VOCs, leadO₃ (tropospheric), NO₂, H₂SO₄, HNO₃, PAN (peroxyacetyl nitrate)
Smog Types
London/Sulphurous Smog: SO₂ + fog + cold weather (reducing type).
Los Angeles/Photochemical Smog: NOₓ + VOCs + sunlight → O₃, PAN (oxidizing type). Occurs in warm, sunny cities with heavy traffic.

Indoor Air Pollution

  • Biomass burning (main cause in rural India) — CO, PM₂.₅.
  • Radon gas from building materials.
  • Asbestos, formaldehyde, VOCs from paints.
  • WHO: Indoor air pollution kills ~3.8 million/year globally.

National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) — India

  • Set by CPCB (Central Pollution Control Board) under EP Act 1986.
  • Two categories: Annual mean and 24-hour mean for each pollutant.
  • Key pollutants: PM₁₀ (100 µg/m³ annual), PM₂.₅ (40 µg/m³ annual), NO₂, SO₂, CO, Ozone, Pb, NH₃, benzene.
  • Revised in 2009; stricter for sensitive zones.

Technology to Mitigate Air Pollution

  • Electrostatic Precipitators (ESP) — charge and collect fly ash from thermal plants.
  • Scrubbers (Wet/Dry) — remove SO₂, HCl, HF from flue gases.
  • Catalytic Converters — vehicles; convert CO, NOₓ, HC to CO₂, N₂, H₂O.
  • Baghouse filters — fabric filters for dust.
  • Fuel switching — CNG, LPG, electric vehicles.
  • BS-VI norms (India, from 2020) — equivalent to Euro VI; reduced PM and NOₓ from vehicles.

💧Water Pollution

▶

Sources of Water Pollution

  • Industrial effluents — heavy metals (Hg, Pb, Cd, As), acids, organic chemicals.
  • Agricultural runoff — pesticides (DDT, BHC), fertilizers (N, P → eutrophication).
  • Sewage/domestic — BOD, pathogens, nitrates, phosphates.
  • Oil spills — marine pollution; kills marine life.
  • Thermal pollution — hot water from power plants.
  • Radioactive waste — nuclear plants, mining tailings.

Key Indicators of Water Quality

Eutrophication

Eutrophication
Excess nutrients (N, P) → algal blooms → algae die → decomposers use up O₂ → hypoxic/dead zones → fish die. Examples: Dal Lake, Hussain Sagar.

Groundwater Pollution — Arsenic Problem

  • Bengal Basin (WB, Bangladesh) — natural arsenic (As) in groundwater from geological sources.
  • Fluoride contamination — Rajasthan; causes fluorosis.
  • Nitrate from fertilizers — "blue baby syndrome" (methemoglobinemia).

Technology to Mitigate Water Pollution

  • Effluent Treatment Plants (ETP) — primary (settling), secondary (biological), tertiary (chemical) treatment.
  • Sewage Treatment Plants (STP) — activated sludge process.
  • Constructed wetlands — natural filtration using plants.
  • Reverse Osmosis (RO) — membrane filtration for drinking water.
  • Zero Liquid Discharge (ZLD) — no effluent release into water bodies.

🔊Noise Pollution & Thermal/Radioactive Pollution

▶
Noise Pollution
Unwanted sound that disrupts normal activity. Unit of measurement: decibel (dB). Measured on logarithmic scale.

Noise Standards (CPCB, India)

AreaDay (6AM–10PM)Night (10PM–6AM)
Industrial75 dB70 dB
Commercial65 dB55 dB
Residential55 dB45 dB
Silence Zone (hospitals/schools)50 dB40 dB

Health Effects of Noise

  • >85 dB — NIHL (Noise-Induced Hearing Loss).
  • >120 dB — pain threshold; structural damage to ear.
  • Physiological: hypertension, tachycardia, sleep disturbance, hormonal stress.
  • Psychological: anxiety, irritability, cognitive impairment.

Thermal Pollution

  • Cooling water from power plants discharged into rivers/lakes → raises water temperature.
  • Effects: reduces DO, disrupts aquatic ecosystems, accelerates algal growth, affects fish reproductive cycles.

Radioactive Pollution

  • Sources: nuclear power plants, weapons testing, medical/industrial radioisotopes, natural radon.
  • Effects: cancers, mutations, radiation sickness, long-term genetic damage.
  • Key incidents: Chernobyl (1986, Ukraine) — worst nuclear disaster; Fukushima (2011, Japan).
  • Units: Becquerel (activity), Gray (absorbed dose), Sievert (effective dose).

National Green Tribunal (NGT)

NGT Facts
  • Established in 2010 under NGT Act 2010; operational since 2011.
  • Headquartered in New Delhi; benches in Bhopal, Pune, Kolkata, Chennai.
  • First specialized environmental tribunal in India.
  • Deals with substantial questions relating to environment including enforcement of legal rights, damages to environment.
  • Applies precautionary principle and polluter pays principle.
  • Consists of a Chairperson + Judicial Members + Expert Members.
  • Appeals against NGT orders go to the Supreme Court.
  • Landmark orders: Yamuna pollution, Ganga cleaning, e-waste management.

Landmark Supreme Court Judgments

  • M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1986–ongoing) — series of cases; CNG for Delhi buses; Ganga pollution; Taj Trapezium Zone.
  • Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. UoI (1996) — established precautionary principle and polluter pays principle as part of Indian law.
  • Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991) — Right to a clean environment is a Fundamental Right under Art. 21.
  • Oleum Gas Leak Case / Shriram Foods (1986) — "Absolute Liability" rule (no exceptions unlike Strict Liability).
  • T.N. Godavarman v. UoI (1996) — expanded definition of "forest"; interim protection of all forests.
  • Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. UoI (1996) — chemical industries causing H-acid pollution; polluter pays.

🇮🇳National Environmental Case Studies

▶

Case Study 1: Bhopal Gas Tragedy (1984)

Critical Case Study
Date: 2–3 December 1984. Location: Union Carbide India Ltd. (UCIL) pesticide plant, Bhopal, MP.
Cause: Water entered a storage tank containing Methyl Isocyanate (MIC) → runaway reaction → release of ~40 tonnes of toxic gas.
Impact: ~3,700 immediate deaths (official); 15,000–20,000 total deaths; 500,000+ injuries; groundwater still contaminated.
Environmental Impact: Soil and groundwater contamination continues decades later.
Legal Outcome: Led to Environment Protection Act 1986; Absolute Liability principle (Shriram Gas Leak case).
Key Lesson: Corporate responsibility; inadequate safety culture; importance of emergency planning.

Case Study 2: Ganga Pollution

  • India's holiest river; 2,525 km long; basin = most populous river basin in world.
  • Pollution sources: 764 million litres/day of sewage; tannery effluents (Kanpur — chromium); industrial discharge.
  • Ganga Action Plan I (1985) — limited success due to poor implementation.
  • Namami Gange Programme (2014) — ₹20,000 crore; 100% central funding; sewage treatment plants.
  • SC has treated cleaning Ganga as highest priority under M.C. Mehta cases.

Case Study 3: Chipko Movement

  • 1973, Chamoli, Uttarakhand; women hugged trees to prevent commercial logging.
  • Led by Sunderlal Bahuguna, Chandi Prasad Bhatt, Gaura Devi.
  • Resulted in Forest Conservation Act 1980.
  • Inspired eco-movements globally; Wangari Maathai's Green Belt Movement in Kenya.

Case Study 4: Narmada Bachao Andolan

  • Protest against Sardar Sarovar Dam and Narmada Valley Development Project.
  • Led by Medha Patkar; supported by Arundhati Roy, Baba Amte.
  • Issues: displacement of 200,000+ tribals; forest submergence; inadequate rehabilitation.
  • SC case (1994–2000): allowed dam height increase with conditions on rehabilitation.

🌐International Environmental Case Studies

▶

Case Study 1: Chernobyl Nuclear Disaster (1986)

  • April 26, 1986; Reactor 4 of Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant, Ukraine (then USSR).
  • Cause: flawed reactor design + operator error during safety test.
  • Released 400× radiation of Hiroshima bomb.
  • 30 km Exclusion Zone; Pripyat city evacuated permanently.
  • Cancer cases (particularly thyroid cancer), deformities in affected regions.
  • Triggered global rethinking of nuclear safety; contributed to USSR collapse.

Case Study 2: Minamata Disease (Japan)

  • 1950s–1960s, Minamata Bay, Japan.
  • Cause: Chisso Corporation dumped methylmercury into bay → bioaccumulated in fish → eaten by humans.
  • Effects: neurological damage, birth defects ("cat dancing disease" in animals), deaths.
  • Led to Minamata Convention on Mercury (2013).

Case Study 3: Ozone Hole — Antarctica

  • Discovered by British Antarctic Survey in 1985 over Antarctica.
  • Cause: CFCs (from refrigerants, aerosols) → stratosphere → UV destroys ozone by catalytic reaction.
  • Response: Montreal Protocol (1987) → CFCs banned globally.
  • Ozone layer showing signs of recovery; full recovery expected by ~2065.

Case Study 4: Amazon Deforestation

  • Amazon = 40% of world's tropical forests; "lungs of the Earth."
  • Brazil lost ~17% of Amazon in 50 years; accelerated under political pressure for agriculture.
  • Tipping point theory: at ~20–25% deforestation → forest may irreversibly convert to savanna.
  • Impacts: biodiversity loss, reduced rainfall, increased GHG.

Solid Waste Management (Campus)

  • Segregation at source — green (wet/biodegradable), blue (dry/recyclable), black (hazardous) bins.
  • Composting — kitchen and canteen waste → organic compost for campus gardens.
  • Biogas plants — food waste → CH₄ for cooking.
  • E-waste collection — old computers/mobiles to certified recyclers.
  • Vermicomposting — earthworm-based decomposition of organic waste.

Water Management (Campus)

  • Rainwater harvesting — rooftop collection → percolation pits → groundwater recharge.
  • Water audits — identify leakages and wastage.
  • Greywater recycling — treated wash water for irrigation.
  • Sewage Treatment Plant (STP) — campus sewage treated → reused for horticulture.
  • Low-flow fixtures — water-saving taps and flush systems.

Campus Biodiversity Documentation

  • Systematic survey: transect walks, quadrat sampling.
  • Record flora (trees, shrubs, herbs, grasses) with scientific names.
  • Fauna: birds (morning counts), insects (pitfall traps), reptiles.
  • Create campus biodiversity register.
  • Identify native vs. invasive species.
  • Prepare biodiversity map of campus.

Field Visit Methodology

  • Pre-visit: Background study, permission, safety briefing.
  • During visit: Systematic observation, data collection (photographs, measurements, species lists).
  • Post-visit: Data analysis, report writing, recommendations.
  • Report format: Introduction → Objective → Methodology → Observations → Analysis → Conclusion.
